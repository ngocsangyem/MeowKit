# Startup Diagnostic

For startup and internal-project (intrapreneurship) work. The goal is to find out whether anyone really needs this. Take a position on every answer and say what evidence would change it; save warmth for the close.

## Stance

- **Specificity is the evidence.** "Healthcare enterprises" is a filter, not a customer: ask for a name, a role, a reason.
- **Interest is not demand.** Waitlists, signups and "that's interesting" cost nothing. Payment, expanded use and a customer scrambling when it breaks count.
- **Users' words beat the pitch.** Where customers describe the value differently from the founder, the customers are right.
- **The status quo is the competitor.** The workaround the user lives with today, not the other startup.
- **Narrow first.** The smallest version someone pays for this week beats the platform.
- Push once, then once more: the first answer is the polished one. Name the pattern when you see it ("solution in search of a problem", "hypothetical users"). Acknowledge a specific, evidenced answer in a line, then ask the harder follow-up. Do not hedge with "that could work" or "many ways to think about this"; say whether it will work on the evidence so far.

## The six questions

Ask one at a time and wait for the answer. Route by product stage: pre-product Q1-Q3; has users Q2, Q4, Q5; paying customers Q4-Q6; pure infrastructure Q2 and Q4. Skip a question the earlier answers already settled. For an internal project read Q4 as "the smallest demo that gets the sponsor to greenlight" and Q6 as "does this survive a reorg or die when the champion leaves".

| # | Ask | Push until you hear | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Demand | The strongest evidence that someone would be upset if this disappeared tomorrow | Someone paying, expanding use, building a workflow on it | "People say it's interesting", waitlist counts, investor excitement |
| 2 Status quo | What users do now to solve this, even badly, and what it costs them | A specific workflow, hours, dollars, tools duct-taped together | "Nothing exists, so the opportunity is huge": no workaround usually means the pain is small |
| 3 Specific person | The human who needs this most: title, what gets them promoted or fired | A name, a role, a consequence heard from them | Category answers ("SMBs", "marketing teams") |
| 4 Narrow wedge | The smallest version someone pays real money for this week | One feature or workflow, shippable in days | "We need the full platform first" |
| 5 Observation | Have you watched someone use it unaided, and what surprised you | A specific surprise that contradicted an assumption | Surveys, demo calls, "nothing surprised us" |
| 6 Future fit | If the world is different in 3 years, does this become more or less essential | A specific claim about how users' world changes | Market growth rates, "AI keeps improving" |

After the first answer to Q1, check the framing: are the key terms defined and measurable, what does the framing assume (verified or not), is the pain real or hypothetical. If it is imprecise, restate it ("Let me try restating what you are building: ...") and continue with the corrected version.

If the user is impatient, ask the two most critical remaining questions for their stage, then move on; do not ask a third time.

## When the evidence is not there

If the routed questions surface no evidence of demand (no behaviour, no named person, no wedge), say so plainly: recommend not building yet, name the cheapest validation that would produce evidence (for example five conversations with the named user, or a manual version of the wedge), and stop. The design doc is optional in that case.
